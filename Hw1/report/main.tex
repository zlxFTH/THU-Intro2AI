\section*{Q1}

\subsection*{Q1.1 Search with Heuristics}

\subsubsection*{(a) Size of the state space}
A state assigns the $n$ labelled cars to $n$ distinct cells of the $n\times n$
grid, so the exact number of states is
\[
    \binom{n^{2}}{n} n! = n^{2}(n^{2}-1)\cdots(n^{2}-n+1) = \Theta(n^{2n}),
\]
i.e.\ $O(n^{2n})$.

\subsubsection*{(b) Admissibility of $h_i$}
With a single car the grid is empty, so the shortest path to the goal is exactly
the Manhattan distance and $h_i$ is admissible. With the other
$n-1$ cars on the grid it is \emph{not} admissible: a stationary car can be
hopped over, covering two cells at cost $1$, so the true cost can be smaller
than $h_i$.
\subsubsection*{(c) Admissible candidates}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llp{0.52\linewidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    candidate & admissible & reason \\
    \midrule
    (i) $\sum_i h_i$       & no  & $n=3$, cars $(1,1),(1,2),(1,3)$: $\sum_i h_i = 6$, true cost $5$ \\
    (ii) $\min_i h_i$      & \textbf{yes} & each car needs at least $\lceil h_j/2\rceil$ moves,
                                            hence $h^{*}\ge n\lceil \min_i h_i/2\rceil \ge \min_i h_i$ \\
    (iii) $\max_i h_i$     & no  & $n=3$, cars $(1,1),(2,3),(1,2)$: $\max_i h_i = 4$, true cost $3$ \\
    (iv) $(h_1,\dots,h_n)$ & no  & componentwise it inherits (b) --- a hop covers two cells at cost
                                   $1$, so $h_i$ may exceed the cost of car $i$; a vector also gives no
                                   total order on the fringe \\
    (v) $n\max_i h_i$      & no  & $n=3$, cars $(1,1),(2,1),(2,2)$: $n\max_i h_i = 12$, true cost $5$ \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\subsection*{Q1.2 Adjusted Heuristics}

\begin{center}

Take the graph above with $h(S)=4$, $h(A)=4$, $h(B)=0$, $h(G)=0$: admissible
($h^{*}=5,4,3,0$) but inconsistent on $S\to B$. Expanding $S$ raises
$h(B)=\max(0,4-3)=1$, so $B$ is filled in first by the long path $S\to B$ with
$g=3$ and is expanded before $A$, i.e.\ with $g=3$ instead of $g^{*}(B)=2$. When
$A$ is expanded afterwards, the shorter path $A\to B$ with $g=2$ can no longer be
extended to $B$, since $B$ is already closed.

\subsection*{Q2.4 A$^*$ search}
The priority is $f = g + h$ with $h$ the Manhattan distance. On \texttt{bigMaze}
A$^*$ expands 549 nodes against 620 for UCS and returns the same optimal path of
cost 210. On the almost empty \texttt{openMaze} the differences are starker:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
    \toprule
    algorithm & nodes expanded & path cost \\
    \midrule
    DFS        & 576 & 298 \\
    BFS        & 682 & 54  \\
    UCS        & 682 & 54  \\
    A$^*$      & 535 & 54  \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Without walls Manhattan is almost the exact remaining cost, so A$^*$ only
expands a narrow cone towards the goal, while BFS/UCS must sweep a whole
diamond; DFS simply gets lucky along one corridor and pays for it with a path
$5.5\times$ longer.

\subsection*{Q2.5 Corners problem}
The state is $(\text{position},\ \text{frozenset of visited corners})$, the only
information the goal test needs; the start state is
$(\text{startingPosition},\ \varnothing)$. BFS then walks the four corners in
the optimal 28 steps on \texttt{tinyCorners} and expands 1966 nodes on
\texttt{mediumCorners} (``just under 2000'').

\begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.78\linewidth]{../screenshot/q5.png}

\needspace{0.68\textheight}
\subsection*{Q2.6 Corners heuristic}
$h$ is the largest Manhattan distance to a corner not yet visited. It is
admissible (every remaining corner must be reached, so the farthest one costs at
least that much) and consistent (a step changes each distance by at most 1, and
stepping onto a corner removes a distance that is then 0). It expands 1136 nodes
instead of 1966, a 42\% reduction, comfortably below the 1200 threshold for full
credit.

\end{center}

\subsection*{Q3.1 Alpha--beta search}
The score is kept in the fixed \texttt{root\_player} perspective, terminal states
are tested before the depth cutoff, and every successor is generated through
\texttt{ctx.child}, so the shared budget is charged exactly once per successor.
\texttt{alpha} is raised when the root player is to move and \texttt{beta} is
lowered otherwise; the loop breaks as soon as $\alpha \ge \beta$.
\texttt{BudgetExceeded} is left to propagate to the iterative-deepening wrapper.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
    depth & minimax successors & Alpha--beta successors & value \\
    \midrule
    2 & 56  & 32  & 6 \\
    3 & 399 & 147 & 9 \\
    4 & 2000 (budget exhausted) & 523 & 6 \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Both searches return the same value at every depth; the saving is what lets
depth 4 complete at all inside the 2000-successor budget.

\subsection*{Q3.2 Evaluation heuristic}
\texttt{evaluate} scores the 69 four-cell windows: an open three for the root
player is $+100$, an open three for the opponent is $-200$, and a window holding
both colours is dead and contributes nothing. Doubling the defensive weight is
what removed the baseline's worst decision.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
    quantity & value \\
    \midrule
    central-column baseline regret & 307.0 \\
    this heuristic                 & 165.0 \\
    reduction                      & 142.0 (46.3\%) \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The remaining regret sits almost entirely in the fifth benchmark position (121

\subsection*{Q3.3 Move ordering}
Columns are ranked in tiers: immediate win, mandatory block, own open three,
opponent open three, own two, then everything else. The line check only reads
\texttt{state.at} from \texttt{state.landing\_row(col)} along the four axes, so
ordering never calls \texttt{ctx.child} and costs no successor budget.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
    \toprule
    quantity & value \\
    \midrule
    left-to-right baseline accuracy & 19.565\% (9/46 pairs) \\
    this ordering                   & 73.913\% (34/46 pairs) \\
    improvement                     & $+54.348\%$ \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Accuracy understates the effect, because better ordering is what makes pruning
possible. Swapping only \texttt{order\_actions} (same search, same heuristic,
same depth) gives:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
    \toprule
    position & depth & left-to-right & this ordering \\
    \midrule
    $(3,2,4,2,5)$           & 4 & budget exhausted & 131 \\
                            & 5 & budget exhausted & 314 \\
    \addlinespace
    $(3,2,3,2,4,1)$         & 4 & 732 & 190 \\
                            & 5 & budget exhausted & 564 \\
    \addlinespace
    $(3,1,4,2,4,2,5,3)$     & 4 & 807 & 96 \\
                            & 5 & budget exhausted & 383 \\
    \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The returned values are identical in every row while the successor count drops
by a factor of three to eight, and depths that exhaust the budget under the
left-to-right order become reachable.
